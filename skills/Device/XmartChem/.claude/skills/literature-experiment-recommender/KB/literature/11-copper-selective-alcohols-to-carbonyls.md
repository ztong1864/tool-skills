# Copper catalysis for highly selective aerobic oxidation of alcohols to aldehydes/ketones†

Di Zhai<sup>a</sup> and Shengming Ma \*<sup>a,b</sup>

Oxidation of alcohols to aldehydes/ketones is a transformation of fundamental importance. Usually stoichiometric amounts of oxidants are required for these types of reactions. Although aerobic oxidations have been studied, there exist challenges: complicated additives, toxic or expensive catalyst(s), a large amount of base or molecular sieves, limited substrate scope, isomerization of $Z \subset = \mathsf { C }$ bonds, etc. Herein, a general and practical room temperature aerobic oxidation of diferent types of alcohols including allenols, alkenols, propargylic alcohols, benzylic alcohols, and aliphatic alcohols in MeCN (acetonitrile) or DCE (1,2-dichloroethane) using readily available and cheap $\mathsf { C u } ( \mathsf { N O } _ { 3 } ) _ { 2 } { \cdot } 3 \mathsf { H } _ { 2 } \mathsf { O }$ and TEMPO (2,2,6,6-tetramethyl-1-piperidinyloxy) or 4-HO-TEMPO as the catalysts forming aldehydes or ketones with an excellent selectivity has been developed. Some thirty to forty mmol scale reactions have been demonstrated.

## Introduction

Aldehydes and ketones are very common and useful chemicals for organic chemistry, cosmetics, medicinal chemistry, and materials science. They are usually synthesized via oxidation of alcohols both in academic laboratories and industries. Traditional protocols for the oxidation of alcohols require at least a stoichiometric amount of certain expensive oxidants such as MnO , CrO , PCC, PDC, IBX, etc., which would yield an equal amount of waste, resulting in a serious problem for isolation of the products and a big burden to the environment.<sup>1</sup> Therefore, much attention has been focused on the eco-friendly aerobic oxidation of alcohols.<sup>1,2</sup>

In 1981, Gilbride and his coworkers<sup>3a</sup> found that there is a high level of copper in the recovered product of the protein from the serum of a horseshoe crab. Later, Linzen and his coworkers<sup>3b</sup> found in the hemocyanins of arthropods and mollusks two copper atoms bound directly to side chains at the site of oxygen binding while a single iron was contained in the heme of hemoglobin. Thus, copper is the metal of choice in nature to carry oxygen. In fact, Semmelhack, Stahl, Iwabuchi, Markó, and their coworkers<sup>4–6</sup> have made very important contributions in the area of using copper as the catalyst for aerobic oxidation of alcohols. In addition, a catalytic amount each of $\mathrm { C u ( M e C N ) _ { 4 } P F _ { 6 } , }$ DBED, and $\mathrm { D M A P } ^ { \mathrm { 7 } }$ or CuCl combined with highly toxic $\mathrm { O s O _ { 4 } } ^ { 8 }$ may also catalyze this reaction, however, a large amount of 4 Å molecular sieves was required, which makes the reaction non-practical in large-scale productions (Scheme 1). Complicated additives, toxic or expensive catalyst(s), high temperature, a large amount of base or molecular sieves, limited substrate scope (for further examples, see also: Tables 3 and 4), serious isomerization of Z CvC bonds,<sup>5c</sup> etc. call for the development of new catalytic recipes. Thus, cost-efective highly selective aerobic oxidation of alcohols with a wide scope and academic/industrial potential at room temperature is of high current interest. The common approach is usually the development of ligands or more expensive copper catalysts with more complicated amine N-oxyls.<sup>4,5</sup> We tried the copper anion efect and observed that these above-mentioned challenges may be addressed by applying copper nitrate and TEMPO as catalysts, oxidizing a very wide scope of alcohols to aldehydes or ketones with an excellent selectivity (Scheme 1).

Control experiments show that terminal propargylic alcohol, 1-(4-methylphenyl)-2-propyn-1-ol 1b, could not be transformed into the corresponding ketone 2b by the most popular copper catalytic systems,<sup>4f,5a,b</sup> most probably due to the basic nature of the reaction conditions (Table 3, entries 1–3). The current method could aford 1-(4-methylphenyl)-2-propyn-1-one 2b in 94% NMR yield (Table $^ { 3 , }$ entry 6). Since terminal alkynones are a type of very useful compounds in organic synthesis, we demonstrated the scope carefully. Besides 4-methylphenyl, other electron-withdrawing and electron-donating group substituted phenyl groups, thienyl and even $n { \mathrm { - C } } _ { 9 } \mathrm { H } _ { 1 9 }$ substituted terminal propargylic alcohols provided the desired alkynones 2b–2h in good to excellent yields (Table 4, entries 1–7).

We also demonstrated the scope of the oxidation reaction of non-terminal propargylic alcohols to the corresponding aldehydes or ketones. When $\mathbf { R } ^ { 2 }$ is Ph, diferent groups including n-Bu, cyclopropyl, Ph and TMS may be introduced as the $\mathbb { R } ^ { 1 }$ group with excellent yields (Table $^ { 5 , }$ entries 1–4, 2a and 2i–2k). $\mathbf { R } ^ { 1 }$ may also be n-Bu with $\mathbf { R } ^ { 2 }$ being diferent electron-withdrawing and electron-donating group substituted phenyl groups, 2-furyl and n-Bu, providing the desired ketones 2l–2t in good to excellent yields (Table 5, entries 5–13). When $\mathbb { R } ^ { 1 }$ is aryl and $\mathbf { R } ^ { 2 }$ is replaced by cyclohexyl, naphthyl and proton, the desired ketones or aldehydes can also be provided in good to excellent yields (Table $^ { 5 , }$ entries 14–17, 2u–2x). The tolerance of synthetically useful functional groups is highlighted in red, demonstrating the potential of this methodology (Tables 4 and 5).

We are also pleased to observe that this protocol may be used in the oxidation of allylic alcohols. Besides good to excellent yields of these reactions, the isomerization of the CvC bond is very limited for both primary alcohols and secondary alcohols (Scheme 2, eqn (1)–(5), 4a–4e).<sup>5c</sup>

To further demonstrate the potential and eficiency of this catalytic system, we conducted some reactions on scales practical in an academic laboratory. The reaction may be conducted smoothly on a 30 mmol scale with 2.5 mol% catalyst loading, giving alkynones 2a in 98% isolated yield (Scheme 4, eqn (1)). Three types of solutions have been provided: using a bag of air (size: 48 $\mathbf { L } ,$ oxygen bags used in hospitals) for 1.5 h first with the subsequent supply of the consumed oxygen with a bag of pure $\mathbf { O } _ { 2 }$ (commercial size: 2 L, may be expanded to 5 L) may eficiently avoid the unsafe high concentration of pure $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ over MeCN, which is critical for industrial scale production (Scheme 4, eqn (2)). The oxidation of 1-(2-thienyl)-2-propyn-1- ol 1g was also conducted on a 40 mmol scale by applying a slow flow of air (30 mL min<sup>−1</sup>),<sup>10</sup> giving 79% isolated yield of 2g (Scheme 4, eqn (3)).

## Conclusions

In conclusion, we have developed an eco-friendly and practical eficient aerobic oxidation of diferent alcohols to aldehydes/ ketones using $\mathrm { C u } ( \mathrm { N O } _ { 3 } ) _ { 2 } { \cdot } 3 \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O }$ and TEMPO as catalysts. This protocol tolerates most common alcohols in organic synthesis such as propargylic alcohols, allylic alcohols, allenols, benzylic alcohols and normal alkanols, providing the corresponding aldehydes/ketones in good to excellent yields with an excellent selectivity. The oxidation reaction may be easily executed on a practical scale in an academic laboratory and no over-oxidation has been observed. Further studies of this copper catalytic system including the application in industrial scale productions are being actively pursued by our group.

Synthesis of 2a with a bag of air with $\mathbf { O } _ { 2 }$ supplemented by a bag of pure $\mathbf { O } _ { 2 } .$ To a 250 mL three-neck flask were added $\mathrm { C u } ( \mathrm { N O } _ { 3 } ) _ { 2 } { \cdot } 3 \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O }$ (968.2 mg, 4.0 mmol), TEMPO (636.7 mg, 4.0 mmol), and 1a (7.5332 g, 40.0 mmol) in MeCN (80 mL) sequentially. After stirring at $2 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for 1.5 h, a 2 L bag of pure $\mathbf { O } _ { 2 }$ was connected to the flask to supplement $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 } .$ The resulting mixture was stirred at 25 $^ \circ \mathrm { C }$ for 4.5 h as monitored by TLC (petroleum ether/ethyl acetate = 5/1), filtered through a short column of silica gel [eluent: ethyl ether (150 mL)], concentrated under reduced pressure, and purified by column chromatography on silica gel [eluent: petroleum ether/ethyl ether = 30/1] to aford 2a (7.4587 $^ { \mathrm { g } , }$ 100%) as an oil.
